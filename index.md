## Core Philosophy: Buy As Much Protection As You Need, While Paying As Little As You Can

Insurance is for protection. It is not something on which we want a return, but an expense for transferring away major risks.

We buy as much protection as we need but pay as little as we can, so that we have enough money left both to live purposefully now and to save for the future. To achieve this, we use cost-effective insurance, especially term insurance for income replacement needs. We avoid using insurance for both protection and saving, since such "two-in-one" structures give insufficient returns due to high embedded distribution (commission) and other costs.

Insurance protects us and our loved ones from the financial aspects of loss in life's worst-case scenarios — it is the foundation of financial resilience. Because money is limited, we should be balanced: sufficient coverage, but not over-paying for events that might not happen. Otherwise we will not have enough left to save and invest towards the future. We also should not seek to use insurance to pay for everything, e.g. all imaginable incidental costs from being sick until the end of our lives.

We cannot predict how needs and costs will change over time. Instead, we use insurance for severe but uncontrollable events, and build up savings and investments as a buffer for the rest — a nest egg that is ours regardless of whether anything goes wrong.

**Recommended premium ceiling:** keep individual life and health insurance premiums under **15% of take-home income** (per MAS's Basic Financial Planning Guide), and ideally below **10%**, to free up money for other purposes.

We achieve this balance by understanding three things:
1. **Core Insurance Cover** — what the core life risks are and which insurance types cover them
2. **Duration** — when we need each type of insurance
3. **Sum Assured** — how much of each insurance we need

Two-in-one insurance policies (whole life plans, endowment plans, investment-linked policies/ILPs) conceptually provide both protection and savings/investment. However, the high embedded cost and structure of these policies mean the net long-term return to policyholders is low, often with a high surrender charge that locks in the investment for years before breakeven — without a return that rewards that long lock-in. (Detailed further below under "Two-in-One Insurance".)

## Core Life Risks and Which Insurance Types Cover Them

Insurance is a way of managing risk. Risk events can be categorised along two dimensions: how controllable they are ("bo bian" — roughly, "can't be helped") and how severe their financial impact would be ("jia lat" — roughly, "very serious"). Different combinations call for different strategies: retain, reduce, avoid, or transfer.

Because money is limited, we cannot insure against everything. **We use insurance — i.e. transfer the risk — only for risks that are both uncontrollable and severe: large medical bills; loss of income (through death, illness, or disability); and large care expenses (through severe disability).** Other risks should be retained, reduced, or avoided through other means (e.g. savings, lifestyle choices).

Longevity risk is a separate key risk, addressed through CPF LIFE and lifelong annuities. While technically insurance products, these are out of scope here and covered in MoneyOwl's separate Retirement Planning Philosophy e-book.

**Core Insurance Cover mapping:**

| Core Need | Insurance Types |
|---|---|
| Large Medical Bills | Hospitalisation & Surgical (H&S) insurance; additional Critical Illness insurance (for bills from alternative treatments) |
| Loss of Income | Life insurance (covering death); Critical Illness (CI) insurance; Occupational Disability Income insurance |
| Large Care Expenses (severe disability) | Long-Term Care (LTC) insurance |

Total & Permanent Disability (TPD) is a common rider to death/terminal-illness coverage, at modest additional premium. Late-stage CI is also often a rider on term life policies, but at significant additional premium.

### Always Start With National Schemes

Always consider national schemes first, even when a scheme is non-compulsory for you — the Government has the economies of scale to negotiate the best premiums for a basic level of cover. Consider "add-ons" to basic national schemes before other types of policies. For H&S specifically, prefer an Integrated Shield Plan (IP) over other hospital payout plans or riders — an IP is part of a government programme insurers commit to, whereas other policies can be withdrawn more easily. Examples of add-ons: Integrated Shield Plans and CareShield Life Supplements, which upsize the basic government scheme with private coverage, partly payable via CPF.

| Need | Government Scheme | Private Insurance |
|---|---|---|
| Large Medical Bills | MediShield Life | Integrated Shield Plan (IP) |
| Loss of Income | Dependants' Protection Scheme (DPS) — Term Life; Home Protection Scheme (HPS) — Mortgage Insurance | Term Life policy; CI policy; Disability Income policy |
| Large Care Expenses | CareShield Life | CareShield Life supplements |

- **DPS**: a term life scheme providing basic protection for death, terminal illness (TI), or TPD. Premiums payable via CPF savings; coverage ends at age 65.
- **HPS**: mortgage-reducing term insurance (sum assured reduces as the mortgage is paid off) protecting HDB homeowners using CPF or cash for loan instalments from losing their home on death, TI, or TPD. Insures to age 65 or until the loan is paid up, whichever is earlier.

## Priority Order for Coverage

For a working adult with dependants:

| Category | Insurance |
|---|---|
| **Core (must get)** | 1. H&S insurance · 2. Life insurance (death) · 3. CI insurance |
| **Recommended (good to have)** | 4. Occupational Disability Income insurance · 5. Long-Term Care insurance |

Not all are relevant at every life stage, and individual circumstances may change the priority. Example: someone with no dependants and no liabilities may prioritise CI insurance over life insurance, since there is no one to provide for on death.

**Personal Accident and early/limited-coverage CI (e.g. cancer-only plans)** are supplementary, not part of this core priority sequence. Personal Accident plans boost death/disability cover cheaply but pay out only for accidents, not illness — consider only if the cost is very low. Since these events may not stop you from working, consider them only after core needs and savings/retirement provisions are set aside.

## When We Need Each Type of Insurance (Duration)

Insurance can start from birth or as early as possible, or only at a new life stage (e.g. starting work). It can end after a fixed "term" (e.g. retirement age 65) or continue to end of life. **The duration of insurance should match the duration of the need** — do not "maximise" the duration of every type of insurance. Premiums for whole life plans or policies extending into old age are much higher than term plans ending at retirement, and over-committing risks ending up both under-protected and under-funding retirement and other goals.

| Need | Start | Until |
|---|---|---|
| Large Medical Bills | Newborn / child | End of life |
| Loss of Income | Beginning of work, or National Service | Retirement age, or last dependant becomes independent (the mortgage-related portion of life insurance runs to the end of the mortgage term) |
| Large Care Expenses | Age 30 | End of life |

**Estimating dependency years in Singapore:** dependent female children — age 22 minus current age; male children — age 25 minus current age (allowing for National Service); parents — age 85 (general life expectancy) minus current age.

### Is term insurance enough for Critical Illness?

CI risk (heart attack, cancer, stroke, etc.) rises with age, raising the question of whether term CI cover is sufficient. The key fact: **major CI-related medical costs — chemotherapy, dialysis, etc. — are covered by MediShield Life and an Integrated Shield Plan (IP)**, which are periodically reviewed by the Ministry of Health. CI insurance's main purpose is therefore to **replace lost income** while unable to work for treatment and recuperation — once retired with no dependants, there is no income left to replace.

For alternative medicines or treatments not covered by an IP, a whole life plan with a CI rider can provide a lump sum — but only after securing a CI term plan to cover income loss first. It's hard to estimate in advance how much additional CI cover such alternative treatments would need.

By the same income-replacement rationale, **parents should cover themselves for CI before buying CI insurance for their children.** If buying CI for a child, consider an early CI or multi-pay CI plan (lets a parent stop work temporarily to care for the child) — but this is a lower priority than the parents' own core cover and other savings/investments.

### Buy Early

Buying while healthy ensures insurability. Once health conditions set in — even mild ones like being overweight or having mild hypertension — you may become uninsurable, face exclusions, or face premium loading (an additional charge based on individual risk; the higher the risk, the higher the loading). IPs should be bought as early as possible, including for a newborn, since you cannot upgrade to a higher ward class later without full medical underwriting — buy the highest class you can afford and downgrade later if needed. (An IP rider is an optional add-on that reduces out-of-pocket expenses.)

It's also generally cheaper to buy insurance younger, and level premiums for personal life/CI insurance can be locked in at a lower rate. Parents wanting cover for their children should still prioritise their own coverage first, balanced against other uses of cashflow.

**Premium structure:** Death/TI/TPD premiums are generally level and guaranteed for the policy term. CI and occupational disability income premiums can be level or non-level (increasing with age) and are non-guaranteed (the insurer may revise them). IP premiums are age-banded and non-guaranteed (increase as the insured ages).

### If budget is tight

MoneyOwl recommends buying insurance covering the full required term. If budget is tight, a short-term (yearly, 5-year, or 10-year) renewable term plan can be a temporary solution — some cover is better than none. These plans start with low premiums that rise significantly at each renewal (based on age at renewal, usually without new medical underwriting), resulting in high total premiums over time. Group insurance premiums also tend to be age-banded, with the exception of MINDEF/MHA Group Term Life, which stays level until age 65.

### Don't rely on employer/group insurance for core coverage

Unless coverage is fully portable and still affordable after leaving the job or group, don't treat employer/group insurance as core coverage. For H&S, hold your own IP even if your employer offers healthcare cover (accepting duplicate coverage while employed) so coverage continues after you leave. Most group plans in Singapore have limited or poor portability, with MINDEF/MHA Group Term Life (for national servicemen and other eligible employees) an exception.

## How Much Insurance Do We Need (Sum Assured)

Estimating the right level of coverage isn't an absolute science — we cannot know in advance when we'll die or get sick, or what costs will be at that time, and individual circumstances and preferences vary. The following are general sizing principles per category.

### Hospitalisation & Surgical (H&S) Insurance

H&S reimburses expenses — it does not pay a lump sum. Get the best affordable H&S/IP coverage to reduce the largest out-of-pocket expenses, and lock in high coverage while healthy, subject to affordability.

MediShield Life (MSL) gives all Singaporeans and PRs access to subsidised B2/C ward care, but doesn't cover everything (e.g. pre-/post-hospitalisation expenses) and caps claim limits for some treatments; the subsidy extent is means-tested. Even if you're happy with B2/C wards, get the lowest "as charged" IP to cover MSL's gaps and to protect against higher bills if your income/wealth disqualifies you from higher subsidy tiers.

Be prepared to downgrade if premiums become unaffordable, and avoid over-consuming medical services simply because you're insured — it drives up premiums for everyone, including your own family.

### Life Insurance (Death, TI, TPD)

Life insurance pays a lump sum on Death, Terminal Illness (TI), or Total Permanent Disability (TPD). TPD riders should be bought alongside basic death/TI cover, since the marginal cost is usually low.

**Sum assured should cover:**
- Dependants' day-to-day expenses until they're independent (or deceased), or until your originally planned retirement age — whichever is later
- Children's tertiary education expenses
- Settling the mortgage on your primary residence
- Debts/other loans, net of assets that could be disposed of on death

For simplicity, daily expenses aren't inflation-adjusted (offset by assuming death occurs immediately after buying the policy, so the sum assured doesn't decline each year you don't die). You can upsize for inflation at modest extra cost via a term plan — cheaper than extending a plan's duration into old age, due to different actuarial assumptions. For children's education, where the timeline is more certain, inflation *is* factored in.

You can hold separate policies with different sums assured/terms matching different needs (e.g. a mortgage ending 10 years post-retirement) — whether it's worth doing depends on cost.

**Rule of thumb:** MAS's Basic Financial Planning Guide suggests **9× annual income** for death sum assured — a reasonable starting point for implementing term coverage without delay, before a more precise calculation is done. Unlike whole life or ILP cash-value plans, there's no heavy penalty for reducing a term plan's sum assured later.

**CI accelerator note:** if a CI "accelerator" rider bundled with life insurance reduces the death benefit when CI is claimed first, size the death sum assured higher — pure death need plus the "CI-riding-on-life" amount. Example: a $1,000,000 death benefit with a $300,000 CI accelerator rider pays $300,000 on CI diagnosis, which then reduces the remaining death benefit to $700,000. To preserve the original $1,000,000 death coverage, the total sum assured should be set at $1,300,000.

### Critical Illness (CI) Insurance

CI pays a lump sum on diagnosis of a late-stage critical illness. **Recommended sum assured: 4× annual income** (MAS's Basic Financial Planning Guide) — reflecting that CI mainly replaces income, and patients typically need around 4 years to undergo treatment and recover (where death doesn't occur soon after diagnosis).

CI can be upsized beyond 4× for those wanting an additional payout for alternative medicines not covered by H&S insurance (e.g. as a CI rider in a whole life plan, since that need is lifelong). CI can also be boosted with limited CI (e.g. cancer-only) or early/multi-stage CI plans — there's no definitive way to estimate the sum assured needed for this, given how costs evolve.

### Occupational Disability Income Insurance

Provides a monthly cash payment replacing income after a waiting period, for as long as the disability persists — terms may reduce payout if you're able to work, including in other occupations. **Sum assured should be as close as possible to your monthly gross income.** Current plans typically cap replacement income at **75% of salary (65% for self-employed persons)**, after a waiting period.

### Long-Term Care / CareShield Life

CareShield Life (CSL) pays a monthly cash amount upon severe disability — defined as inability to perform 3 of 6 Activities of Daily Living (ADLs): Washing, Dressing, Feeding, Toileting, Mobility, and Transferring.

CSL is mandatory for Singaporean citizens/PRs born from 1 October 2020, once they reach age 30; premiums are fully payable from CPF MediSave. Those born earlier may opt in.

At inception (2020), payouts started at $600/month, about 25% of median nursing home fees before Government subsidy. CareShield Life Supplements from private insurers upscale this amount and can be paid via MediSave up to a certain limit. The payout level depends on the year severe disability occurs, then stays level thereafter — the monthly payout started at $600 (2020), rising 2% a year to $662 (2025). As of 2024, estimated nursing home fees range from $2,200 to $4,200/month before Government subsidy (per the Agency for Integrated Care, AIC).

We recommend opting in to CareShield Life where it isn't compulsory. Whether a Supplement is needed depends on your overall retirement income situation and your expected level of care (day care vs. home-based care vs. nursing home). Severe disability adds expenses, but it also frees up retirement income normally spent on an active lifestyle to cover them — and severe disability may separately trigger a TPD lump sum if a TPD rider was added to life insurance and its definition is met.

Subject to the above and your budget, consider a Supplement triggered at inability to perform 3 ADLs, with benefits increasing yearly for inflation. Plans that pay out at just 1 ADL cost more and may be poorer value, since severe disability tends to trigger several ADLs at once — which is why CSL uses the 3-ADL threshold.

## "Two-in-One" Insurance: Whole Life, Endowment, and ILPs

Whole life, endowment, and investment-linked policies (ILPs) appear to achieve two goals at once — protection and wealth building. In practice they tend to fall short on both, largely because the policies currently sold in Singapore carry embedded costs that are too high (not primarily a structural flaw). Some protection needs can still be met by a whole life plan, and individual, competitively-priced whole life or endowment policies may have a sufficient return. Some retirement income plans (endowment-like) may also suit as a supplement to CPF LIFE. For ILPs, MoneyOwl has not found suitable low-cost policies among current products available in Singapore.

### Whole Life and Endowment Plans (Participating Policies)

These are participating life policies — you "participate" in the returns of the insurer's participating (par) fund, currently illustrated at 4.25% p.a. and 3.00% p.a., though these are **not actual or guaranteed returns**. You participate through insurer-declared bonuses added to both your sum assured and your policy value.

A common misconception: because these plans have a cash value, they seem more worthwhile than term insurance, since "you always get something back" — whereas term premiums are "wasted" if you don't die or get CI within the term. In reality, part of the premium (illustratively, around 20 cents of every dollar) goes to mortality/morbidity charges and is never returned. The remaining ~80 cents is invested by the insurer on your behalf in the par fund — that's the only source of any "return." You only "make money" on the insurance portion if you die or fall ill early, much like term insurance.

**Issue 1 — affordability of full coverage.** It's nearly impossible for most people to fully cover their protection needs using whole life or endowment alone. Many whole life plans add a "multiplier" during working years (effectively a term add-on) in recognition of this, but the required outlay remains large — achieving a 9× income sum assured via term requires only a fraction of the outlay a whole life plan would need.

One argument for whole life: its cash value lets you borrow against the policy to avoid lapsing if you lose your job, which term doesn't allow. Counter-argument: that very risk — being unable to afford premiums — is created by whole life/endowment's high required outlay in the first place.

**Issue 2 — fund returns.** An advantage of whole life/endowment: smoothing — insurers try to keep policyholder bonuses consistent even when the underlying par fund experiences volatility. A more controversial "advantage": high surrender deductions mean the breakeven point (surrender value equalling premiums paid, unadjusted for inflation) can be more than a decade away, which creates discipline to stay invested. MoneyOwl agrees smoothing and this psychological "lock-in" genuinely help low-risk-appetite investors stay invested — the problem is that many such plans mean staying invested at low returns over a long period, due to high embedded costs.

Even at the higher illustrated return (4.25% p.a.), the actual return to the policyholder can be substantially lower — by roughly 1–2% p.a. — due to costs. Beyond insurance costs, policyholders share par fund returns with the insurer's shareholders on a 90:10 basis, with some return used to build reserves; the most significant cost is distribution (marketing and adviser/agent commissions).

**Note:** MoneyOwl's philosophy is **not** a blanket "buy term, invest the rest" (BTIR). BTIR is often criticised because not everyone has the risk appetite, expertise, or discipline for market investing — MoneyOwl believes that with the right knowledge, guidance, and advice, most people can invest without excessive stress, but this doesn't make whole life/endowment the answer instead. Even without comparing to market investing, whole life/endowment returns and breakeven points often compare unfavourably to risk-free options like Singapore Savings Bonds and CPF top-ups. The actual reason to buy term is that, for most people, it's the only affordable way to achieve full protection coverage for income-replacement needs — protection being insurance's first and core purpose, not a comment on what to do with any "savings."

**Where whole life can still fit:** some needs can only be met by a whole life plan — most notably, funding alternative CI medicines/treatments not currently covered by an IP, which is a whole-of-life need. Individual, competitively-priced whole life or endowment policies may still offer a sufficient return, and retirement income policies (an endowment type) may work as an additional income layer above CPF LIFE — these require case-by-case assessment of net return and policy structure.

### Investment-Linked Policies (ILPs)

Unlike participating plans, ILPs place all investment risk on the policyholder, with no smoothing. ILPs have gained traction partly because newer regulations require insurers to hold more capital behind par fund guarantees, making ILPs relatively more attractive to insurers from a business standpoint.

There are two ILP generations: older ones with a larger protection element, and newer ones that are mainly investment-focused. For both, the key issue is cost — investments are restricted to the insurer's own ILP sub-fund list.

- **Older-generation ILPs**: a portion of premium funds an age-banded protection plan, with mortality/morbidity charges rising over time and consuming more of the premium as you age; the rest is invested. At some point, premiums may become insufficient to cover insurance costs, requiring investment units to be sold off to make up the shortfall.
- **Newer-generation ILPs**: lightly wrapped, with a low protection element — on death, the policyholder receives the invested market value plus 1–5%, floored at premiums paid. Some insurers don't charge for this small protection element.

Most insurers have dropped "fund switching" charges, and commonly offer "bonus units" (up to 50% extra) to boost returns in exchange for a minimum investment period that can run for decades — surrendering before that period incurs significant claw-back penalties (up to 50% of units). As with whole life/endowment, the benefit of a long commitment period is undercut by very high ILP costs in Singapore, even after bonus units.

Commissions are still paid, with some later-year commissions structured as a perpetual stream of "trailer commissions" (investment-fund-level expenses) — charged as both upfront and ongoing premium deductions. Policy-level maintenance costs can run to several percentage points a year, and fund-level expenses can also be high, since ILP sub-funds tend to be actively managed with high Total Expense Ratios (TER).

MoneyOwl's investment philosophy is to keep core investments in low-cost, globally diversified, market-following portfolios. To date, MoneyOwl has not found low-cost ILPs fit for purpose — for either protection or investment — among products currently available in Singapore.

## Closing Thoughts

We may feel "over-insured," but the better question is often whether we're overpaying for insurance while still under-protected — data across Singapore shows large protection gaps persist. Insurance needn't be costly if fit-for-purpose instruments are used. The key is remembering insurance is for protection, not returns, unlike investments — premiums paid shouldn't be seen as "wasted" if unclaimed, since we should hope to never need the payouts (that would mean nothing catastrophic happened). A financial plan should be built on having enough surplus for purposeful living, working, and building for one's family while being protected — not on the premise of dying or falling ill.
